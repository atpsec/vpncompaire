Free Wi-Fi at an airport lounge, downtown cafe or hotel lobby is convenient — but on most public networks your traffic is not encrypted by default. Anyone on the same network, a man-in-the-middle attacker, or a fake access point (evil twin) can intercept unprotected data. A VPN does not eliminate every risk, but used correctly it is one of the most practical layers on public Wi-Fi.

## What Are the Real Risks?

Typical threats on open networks:

- **Sniffing** — unencrypted HTTP, email or form data can be read in transit.
- **Fake hotspots** — copycat networks named like "Free_Airport_WiFi".
- **Session hijacking** — stolen cookies or tokens used to access accounts.
- **DNS manipulation** — fake login or banking pages via rogue DNS.

HTTPS is standard on major sites, but app updates, ad networks and legacy protocols still leave gaps. A [DNS leak test](/blog/dns-leak-test-vpn) confirms DNS queries do not bypass your VPN to the ISP.

## How VPN Reduces Exposure

A VPN wraps outbound traffic in an encrypted tunnel. A listener on the same Wi-Fi sees a connection to a VPN server, not readable packet contents. This matters especially for:

- Apps that do not enforce HTTPS everywhere
- Networks whose logging policy you do not know
- [Travel scenarios](/blog/vpn-for-travel) at hotels and airports

VPN does **not** remove malware, stop you from clicking phishing links, or automatically block evil-twin networks.

## Practical Checklist

1. **Disable auto-join** on unknown networks.
2. **Connect VPN before sensitive use** — enable [kill switch](/blog/vpn-kill-switch-explained) where available.
3. **Avoid non-HTTPS sites** — check the padlock in the address bar.
4. **Use mobile data for banking** when possible — 4G/5G is often safer.
5. **Turn off file sharing and AirDrop** on shared networks.
6. **Use an up-to-date client** — modern WireGuard or OpenVPN apps.
