## Abstract

A free virtual private network (VPN) can encrypt traffic between a device and a tunnel endpoint, but it does not remove the need to trust the organization operating that endpoint. The provider may be able to observe connection metadata, apply traffic policies, collect device identifiers, or finance the service through advertising and data sharing. A VPN also does not prevent phishing, malware, account takeover, or tracking performed by websites to which a user is logged in.

This article reviews the main risks without evaluating or naming a particular VPN brand. It combines regulatory guidance, an historical academic measurement study, and a recent independent transparency audit. The evidence is interpreted cautiously: a policy violation is not proof that every user was harmed, and a clean leak test is not proof that a provider keeps no records. The practical objective is to help readers make a proportionate decision before installing a free VPN.

> **Key finding:** “Free” describes the price paid by the user; it does not describe the amount of trust, data, or technical exposure involved. The relevant questions are what the service can technically access, what it says it collects, how long it retains information, and whether those statements are independently verifiable.

## 1. What changes when a VPN is enabled?

A VPN creates a virtual network interface and sends traffic through an encrypted tunnel to a gateway operated by the VPN service. The local Wi-Fi operator and the internet service provider may see less of the destination traffic, while the VPN gateway becomes a new point of trust.

~~~text
Device → VPN application → Encrypted tunnel → VPN gateway → Website or online service
~~~

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) explains that a VPN application can receive permission to intercept all internet traffic from a device. Its consumer guidance also warns that some applications have failed to encrypt traffic, requested sensitive permissions, or shared information with third parties. A VPN therefore changes the observer that can see network activity; it does not make the user anonymous. Read the [FTC guidance on VPN applications](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2018/02/market-vpn-app) for the regulatory context.

The tunnel normally protects the device-to-gateway segment. HTTPS should still protect the application-layer connection between the gateway and the destination website. This distinction matters:

- A VPN can reduce exposure to an untrusted local network.
- The VPN operator can still process connection metadata and, depending on the application and protocol, may be able to inspect unencrypted traffic.
- The destination can still identify a user through an account, cookies, browser characteristics, or other application signals.
- A VPN does not clean an infected device or repair a compromised account.

The security benefit is therefore conditional on correct implementation, correct use, and a provider whose data practices match the user’s expectations.

## 2. Why would a free service collect data?

Operating VPN gateways requires servers, bandwidth, engineering, abuse handling, and security maintenance. A free plan can be financed by a paid tier, donations, grants, advertising, analytics, or by monetizing some part of the service’s data and network capacity. None of these models is automatically unlawful or unsafe, but the model should be stated clearly enough for a user to evaluate it.

### Advertising identifiers and profiling

Advertising-supported applications may process an advertising identifier, IP address, approximate location, device model, operating system, and in-app events. Each field can appear low-risk in isolation. When combined over time, the fields can form a persistent usage profile. The presence of advertising does not by itself demonstrate that browsing content is sold; it does mean the privacy policy should explain the advertising partners, identifiers, purposes, retention, and opt-out choices.

Look for precise terms such as **traffic data**, **usage data**, **device identifiers**, **diagnostics**, **partners**, and **personalized advertising**. A statement that data is “anonymous” should be accompanied by a description of which fields are transformed, whether re-identification is assessed, and whether the data is joined with another dataset.

### Analytics and operational telemetry

Providers commonly collect some telemetry to detect crashes, measure performance, limit abuse, or plan capacity. The important questions are scope and retention:

- Are full URLs, DNS queries, or only aggregate byte counts processed?
- Are timestamps stored with an account, device, or IP address?
- Is the free plan governed by a different policy from the paid plan?
- Are analytics processors or advertising partners named?
- Is there a documented deletion request process?

A short “no logs” slogan cannot answer these questions. A defensible policy defines the data categories, the purpose for each category, the retention period, and the legal basis or consent mechanism where applicable.

### Freemium limits and behavior nudges

A limited plan can be a legitimate way to introduce a service. Typical limits include a small server set, a data quota, lower priority at busy times, or restricted protocol choices. The commercial risk is surprise rather than the existence of a limit. Before installation, check the quota, the renewal terms, whether a trial converts automatically, and whether a connection interruption leaves traffic unprotected.

### Bandwidth sharing and residential proxying

Some applications may allow customer devices to act as exit points for other users. This can be described as bandwidth sharing, a peer network, or residential proxy access. It is materially different from an ordinary VPN tunnel because requests from another party may appear to originate from the customer’s IP address.

Do not enable such a feature unless the scope, consent, compensation, abuse handling, and opt-out process are explicit. An unexpected abuse report or a blocked address does not prove that the device was compromised, but it can create a practical and legal burden for the subscriber.

## 3. What does the available evidence show?

Different sources measure different phenomena. Regulatory guidance describes expected disclosures and prohibited practices; academic work measures applications at a particular point in time; a transparency audit evaluates whether an operator can be identified and understood. These forms of evidence should not be treated as interchangeable.

### Regulatory guidance

The FTC notes that a VPN application can intercept device traffic and that some applications have failed to encrypt traffic or have shared information with third parties. This is a warning about the power of the permission and the need to examine the provider’s practices, not a claim that every free VPN behaves identically.

Google Play’s [VpnService policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/12564964?hl=en) requires applications using the platform VPN service to disclose the use, encrypt data from the device to the tunnel endpoint, and provide prominent disclosure and consent for relevant collection or sharing. The policy also restricts redirecting or manipulating traffic for monetization. Store rules establish a baseline; appearing in a store is not equivalent to a complete independent security audit.

### Historical measurement study

A study by researchers from CSIRO, UNSW, ICSI, and UC Berkeley examined 283 Android VPN applications. The authors reported that more than 38% showed a malware signal in VirusTotal at the time of the study. The [peer-reviewed measurement paper (PDF)](https://research.csiro.au/isp/wp-content/uploads/sites/106/2016/08/paper-1.pdf) is important historical evidence, but it should not be read as a current prevalence estimate for all applications. Application populations, store controls, and malware scanners change. The study demonstrates why VPN permissions and distribution channels deserve scrutiny.

### Recent transparency audit

A 2026 TechRadar audit examined 3,471 Android VPN applications and reported that 43% lacked a professional website, 63% used a personal email address, and 54% had a privacy policy that the audit considered substandard. The [published audit](https://www.techradar.com/vpn/investigation-over-75-percent-of-android-vpns-fail-basic-transparency-tests) measures transparency and accessibility. It does not establish that every application in the sample contains malware, logs identifiable browsing histories, or violates a specific law. Its value is as a screening signal: an operator that cannot be identified or whose policy cannot be read is difficult to hold accountable.

## 4. Technical risk taxonomy

### Interception and retention

The VPN application is positioned to route traffic from the device. Depending on the protocol and configuration, the provider can usually observe at least the subscriber’s IP address, connection times, amount of traffic, selected gateway, and destination IP or domain metadata. Unencrypted application traffic may also be readable. The provider’s ability to access a field and its decision to retain that field are separate questions.

### Incomplete encryption

The VPN icon does not prove that every packet is encrypted. Split tunneling can intentionally exclude selected applications. A misconfigured DNS resolver, an IPv6 path outside the tunnel, or a reconnect interval can expose traffic temporarily. The implementation should document the protocol, cipher suite, certificate or key handling, and what happens when the tunnel fails.

### DNS, IPv6, and WebRTC leakage

A DNS leak occurs when name-resolution queries leave through a resolver that the user did not intend to use. An IPv6 leak occurs when IPv6 traffic bypasses an IPv4-only tunnel. WebRTC can reveal local or public network candidates to a browser application under some configurations. Test these channels separately:

1. Record the IP, DNS, and IPv6 results with the VPN disabled.
2. Repeat the same tests with the VPN enabled and the same browser profile.
3. Change the VPN gateway and repeat the measurements.
4. Disconnect the tunnel and observe whether traffic is blocked or simply sent directly.

A clean result shows what happened during the test. It does not establish that the provider keeps no logs or that future application updates will preserve the same behavior. Our [DNS leak test guide](/blog/dns-leak-test-vpn) and [VPN privacy and security guide](/blog/vpn-privacy-and-security) explain the limits of these checks.

### Excessive permissions

The VPN service permission is expected for a VPN implementation. Other permissions should have a specific, understandable purpose. Requests for contacts, SMS, microphone, accessibility services, or broad file access are not automatically malicious, but they expand the consequences of a compromise and conflict with data-minimization principles when no clear function requires them.

| Permission or capability | Plausible purpose | Question to ask |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VPN service | Creating the virtual tunnel interface | Does it route all traffic or only selected applications? |
| Notifications | Showing connection state | Are notifications used for persistent promotions? |
| Approximate location | Selecting a nearby gateway | Is location collected continuously or only during selection? |
| Device identifier | Account support or diagnostics | Is it linked to advertising or shared with partners? |
| Contacts, SMS, microphone, accessibility | No general VPN requirement | Does the core connection work when access is denied? |

### Malware and supply-chain exposure

A VPN application with malware can be more consequential than an ordinary utility because it occupies a privileged network position. Potential outcomes include adware, credential theft, covert remote access, resource abuse, or unauthorized traffic redirection. Risk also enters through cloned listings, modified APK files, unofficial download pages, and abandoned libraries.

Install only from an official distribution channel, verify the developer identity, keep the operating system current, and avoid “premium unlocked” or modified packages. A store listing reduces some distribution risks but cannot prove that the current binary has no vulnerability.

### Reputation, blocking, and performance

A shared exit address may already be associated with abuse, automation, or copyright complaints. Websites can challenge or block the address even when the subscriber has done nothing wrong. Free capacity constraints can also create congestion, latency, and repeated reconnects. Performance is not merely a convenience issue: a tunnel that drops frequently can cause applications to fall back to a direct connection or encourage users to disable protective settings.

## 5. Which data may be exposed?

The following categories are commonly relevant during a VPN session. The exact scope depends on the application, operating system, protocol, and provider policy.

| Data category | How it may arise | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Source IP and approximate location | Tunnel registration | Can link sessions or reveal a geographic region |
| DNS queries and destination metadata | Name resolution and routing | Can reveal interests, services, and timing |
| Device and advertising identifiers | SDKs, diagnostics, account support | Enable persistence across sessions or apps |
| Session time, server, and byte count | Capacity and abuse controls | Can build a behavioral pattern |
| Account email and support messages | Registration or troubleshooting | Directly identifies the subscriber |
| Cookies and login state | Destination websites | Defeat anonymity when a user is authenticated |

Pseudonymous data can still be personal data when it can be linked back to a device, account, or recurring pattern. Read the policy for collection, sharing, retention, deletion, and legal-disclosure procedures rather than relying on a single label.

## 6. What a VPN does not solve

A VPN is one network control. It does not replace layered security.

| Threat | VPN limitation | Additional control |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Phishing | Does not verify that a page is genuine | Type important addresses manually and use MFA |
| Malware | Does not sanitize downloads or a compromised device | Patch promptly and use reputable endpoint protection |
| Account takeover | Does not repair a reused or stolen password | Use a password manager and unique credentials |
| Browser fingerprinting | IP rotation does not remove browser signals | Update the browser and reduce unnecessary tracking |
| Stolen cookies | Does not revoke an existing session | Sign out of all sessions and rotate credentials |
| Provider-side logging | Cannot override the operator’s policy | Examine retention, audits, and deletion procedures |
| Unsafe public Wi-Fi | Does not detect a fake access point | Use HTTPS, disable auto-join, and verify the network |

For banking, government, work, and health accounts, HTTPS, multi-factor authentication, device updates, and account alerts remain necessary even when a VPN is enabled.

## 7. A reproducible evaluation protocol

Use the following protocol before trusting a free VPN with sensitive traffic:

1. **Identify the operator.** Record the legal entity, support channel, official domain, and developer account.
2. **Archive the privacy policy.** Save the version date and check it again after major updates.
3. **Map data categories.** Mark every collection, sharing, retention, and deletion statement.
4. **Compare plans.** Check whether the free tier has different processing rules from paid tiers.
5. **Inspect permissions.** Deny unrelated access and verify that basic connectivity still works.
6. **Check distribution.** Avoid unofficial APKs and cloned listings.
7. **Record technical claims.** Note the protocol, encryption, DNS handling, IPv6 support, and kill-switch behavior.
8. **Run leak tests.** Test IP, DNS, IPv6, and WebRTC before and after connection.
9. **Test failure behavior.** Disable the network or stop the VPN service and confirm whether traffic is blocked.
10. **Measure stability.** Repeat tests across gateways and time periods; log disconnects and latency.
11. **Look for independent evidence.** Prefer an audit with a named scope, date, methodology, and report.
12. **Evaluate the business model.** Ask who pays for infrastructure and whether bandwidth sharing is enabled.
13. **Review incident handling.** Find a security contact and prior disclosure history.
14. **Check cancellation and deletion.** Confirm that subscriptions can be cancelled and account data can be requested for deletion.
15. **Limit exposure.** Start with low-risk browsing and do not use the service for credentials until evidence is sufficient.

A checklist with many unanswered items is itself a risk signal. Download counts, star ratings, and a large server list are popularity indicators; they are not evidence of a no-logging architecture.

## 8. If a free VPN is unavoidable

For a short, low-risk session, reduce exposure in this order:

1. Download from the official store and confirm the developer identity.
2. Read and save the current privacy policy before granting the VPN permission.
3. Use a separate browser profile or test device when practical.
4. Deny unrelated permissions and stop if the core connection requires them.
5. Enable a kill switch or “block connections without VPN” setting.
6. Run IP, DNS, IPv6, and WebRTC tests before entering any sensitive information.
7. Keep HTTPS, MFA, and unique passwords enabled.
8. Avoid banking, workplace administration, health records, and other high-impact accounts until the provider is independently verifiable.
9. Remove the application if it injects advertisements into pages, redirects traffic, overheats the device, or causes unexplained battery and data use.
10. After use, remove the VPN profile and review notification, accessibility, and device-administrator settings.

These measures reduce exposure; they do not convert an unverified service into a verified one.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are all free VPNs unsafe?

No. Some free plans can be reasonable for limited, low-risk use. Price alone provides no assurance. Assess the operator, data policy, technical behavior, permissions, and independent evidence together.

### Can a free VPN provider see my internet traffic?

The provider can usually observe traffic entering and leaving its gateway, including connection metadata. HTTPS can protect content between the gateway and a website, but it does not prevent the provider from retaining timing, destination, account, or device information if its systems collect those fields.

### Do free VPNs sell user data?

There is no single answer for every service. The FTC has warned that some VPN applications show advertising or share information with third parties. Read the current policy for the specific service and distinguish operational telemetry from advertising or data-broker sharing.

### Does a VPN make me anonymous?

No. An IP address can change while a logged-in account, cookie, payment record, browser fingerprint, or provider-side record continues to identify the session. A VPN is a privacy control, not an anonymity guarantee.

### Is public Wi-Fi safe when a VPN is enabled?

A correctly configured VPN can reduce exposure to observers on the local network. It does not identify a fake hotspot, stop phishing, remove malware, or protect a stolen password. HTTPS and MFA are still required.

### How should I test for leaks?

Compare IP, DNS, IPv6, and WebRTC results with the VPN off and on, using the same browser profile. Repeat after changing gateways and after reconnecting. Treat the result as a snapshot of behavior, not proof of a provider’s internal retention practices.

### Does uninstalling the app delete data held by the provider?

Usually, uninstalling removes local files and the VPN profile. It cannot automatically erase an account record, analytics event, or server log already stored by the provider. Follow the policy’s deletion procedure and keep confirmation of the request.

### Can I use a free VPN for banking?

Avoid using an unverified free VPN for banking or other high-impact services. Use the official banking application, HTTPS, MFA, device updates, and a trusted network. If a VPN is required, verify its policy and failure behavior before signing in.

## Conclusion

The central risk of a free VPN is the transfer of network trust to an operator whose incentives and controls may be unclear. Advertising, telemetry, excessive permissions, incomplete encryption, DNS or IPv6 leaks, malicious packages, and bandwidth sharing are distinct risks that require distinct checks. No single store badge, speed test, or “no logs” phrase can resolve them.

A defensible decision follows three steps: read the data policy, test the technical behavior, and keep layered account and device security in place. If the operator cannot explain what it collects, why it collects it, how long it retains it, and how a user can request deletion, do not place personal or financial traffic inside the tunnel.
